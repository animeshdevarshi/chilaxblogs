Every household runs on a hundred small lists. What to buy. What is running low.
What needs fixing before the guests arrive. Who is bringing what on Saturday.
What to pack for the trip. Most of these lists are never written down at all —
they live in one person's head, and that person is quietly tired.

The ones that *do* get written down usually end up in the same place: a single
phone. A note only one person can edit. A message thread that scrolled out of
view three days ago. A list that is, in theory, for the whole family and, in
practice, for whoever remembered to open the app.

## "Shared" usually means "visible to me when I ask"

You are standing in the store. Somewhere in a chat thread this week there were
six things — coffee, dish soap, something for the kids, a birthday card, and two
you have already forgotten. You scroll up past photos and plans and a voice note,
trying to reconstruct a shopping list from a conversation that was never meant to
be one.

A conversation is not a list. It has no state. Nothing is checked off, nothing is
current, and the moment two people are talking, the important line moves up and
away.

## One person becomes the bottleneck

In most homes there is a keeper of the list. They notice the milk is low. They
remember the school form. They hold the running total of what the house needs,
and everyone else asks *them* instead of checking anything.

It works until that person is on a call, or travelling, or simply done being the
household's database. The load is invisible right up until it drops, and then
three things get bought twice and one important thing not at all.

## The scroll

Even when the list exists, it is often the wrong shape. A trip packing list has
sixty lines covering four people, and you need the four that are yours. A house
projects list mixes "buy stamps" with "re-grout the bathroom." You open it, thumb
past everything that is not for you right now, and close it slightly annoyed. Do
that enough times and you stop opening it.

## The trip everyone half-packed

Group travel is the pure form of this problem. Everybody packs the obvious
personal things. Nobody is sure who owns the shared things. Two people bring a
first-aid kit. Nobody brings the power adapter. There was a plan, loosely, spread
across a chat, a couple of memories, and one person's assumption that someone
else had it.

## We have tried everything

The notes app — but sharing is clunky, or it only truly syncs to your own
devices. A group chat — but see above, it is a conversation. A shared document —
but nobody opens a document to add "eggs." A dedicated app — you found a good one,
you were excited, your family installed it, opened it once, and never again.

That last one stings the most, because the app was fine. The problem was never
the feature list.

## Why this stays broken

Most planning tools are built for one of two people: the individual optimising
their own productivity, or the work team with a project manager and a stake in
the process. A household is neither. It is a small group with wildly unequal
enthusiasm for apps, genuinely shared responsibilities, and zero patience for
anything that calls itself "onboarding."

The bar is unforgiving. If the shared list is even slightly more effort than
sending a text, the least app-inclined person in the house will quietly opt out.
Once one person opts out, the list is no longer shared — it is just one more thing
living on one more phone, and you are back where you started.

## What "solved" would feel like

You add "coffee" in about five seconds. Anyone who shops sees it, wherever they
are. Whoever picks it up checks it off, and it is gone for everyone — no
duplicate, no "did you get this?" text. Nobody had to make an account, learn a
layout, or be nagged into checking the app. The list is simply *there*, the way
the fridge is there.

That is a short sentence to write and a stubborn thing to build. It is the part
we think about most.
